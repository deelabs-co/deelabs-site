A foreign software vendor once told us about a lost Thai deal. Great first meeting, strong deck, follow-up emails sent weekly. Six weeks of silence. The deal went to a competitor. When they finally asked what happened, the answer was simple: the competitor's lead had added the buyer on LINE on day one, and the whole evaluation had continued there — questions answered in minutes, a PDF shared into the chat, a follow-up demo arranged in three messages.

Nothing in that story is about software quality. It is about the medium where Thai decisions actually happen.

## Email opens the door. LINE is the room.

In Thai B2B, the pattern we see over and over: discovery happens in email or a formal meeting, but *the deal lives in LINE*. Group chats form around an evaluation — the buyer, their deputy, sometimes a finance person, sometimes the IT gatekeeper. Questions are asked in the chat. Documents are sent into the chat. And, crucially, small signals of intent or hesitation appear there first, long before they would surface in a scheduled call.

A vendor who insists on "moving everything to email" is asking the buyer to leave the room where their own decision-making is happening. Some will, for protocol reasons. Most will simply drift toward the vendor who stayed in the room.

## What LINE-native selling looks like

| Email-era habit | LINE-era equivalent |
|---|---|
| Weekly status emails | Same-day reply in chat, short |
| Formal proposal PDF, then wait | One-page summary in chat, deep doc on request |
| "Shall we schedule a call?" | "Anything blocking? Happy to jump on a call today" |
| Long feature explanations | One diagram, one message |
| Waiting for the buyer to re-engage | Gentle, specific follow-ups with new information |

None of this means being casual about substance. The one-page summaries, the diagrams, and the honest answers must still be excellent. It means delivering the substance in the channel and the rhythm the Thai buyer uses.

## What buyers are actually testing in the chat

Savvy Thai buyers are not just using LINE for convenience. They are running quiet checks:

- **Responsiveness.** How long do you take to answer a real question? That is a preview of support after signing.
- **Straightness.** When asked something you don't know — a PDPA data-flow detail, an integration constraint — do you say "let me check" or do you improvise? Improvising once in a chat can quietly end an evaluation.
- **Respect for hierarchy.** In a group chat with the CFO's deputy present, tone matters. Answer the person who asked; brief the room.

## Automating the first touch, not the relationship

There is a place for automation on the vendor side too: a LINE bot that captures inquiries, answers standard questions about scope and pricing structure, books the first call, and routes the rest to a human. We build these for clients, and the pattern holds — the bot earns attention by being fast and accurate; the human earns the deal by being present when it matters. What fails is trying to automate the *relationship*: Thai buyers notice within days whether a human is actually behind the account.

## A note on first contact: the bot is the new receptionist

Everything above concerns the deal after an intro. But the intro itself increasingly happens on LINE too — a customer or prospect messages the company account at 9 p.m. on a Tuesday, and what happens next sets the tone for everything. If nobody answers until Thursday, the evaluation may be over before it began. This is why the intake side — a bot that captures the inquiry, answers the standard questions, books the first call, and hands everything else to a human — has become the most requested automation we build. It is not glamorous, but it is the front desk of the whole commercial relationship, and front desks that are closed two-thirds of the week cost deals silently.

## Three practical rules

1. **Ask for LINE in the first meeting** — not at the follow-up. "What's the best LINE for your team?" is a normal, polite question in Thailand.
2. **Keep one thread of record.** Chat moves fast; confirm decisions in a short written summary (even in the same chat) so scope never depends on anyone's memory of a voice note.
3. **Respect the rhythm, not just the channel.** Thai business moves on relationships sustained over time. Steady, useful, low-pressure presence beats both silence and pressure.
